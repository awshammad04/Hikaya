## 1. Short overview
A cultural landscape of farmed valleys south-west of Jerusalem, with dry-stone terraces, irrigation channels and a water-sharing system still kept by the families of Battir.

## 3. Cultural and religious importance
The dry-stone terraces show how people settled near springs and turned a steep valley into farmland, using one of the oldest farming methods known to humankind. UNESCO calls the families’ water distribution an ancient, egalitarian system based on a simple mathematical calculation and a clear rotation.

## 4. Main landmarks
- Farmed valleys (widian) lined with dry-stone terraces
- Irrigated vegetable terraces and dry terraces of vines and olives
- The spring-fed collection pool and irrigation channels
- Rock-cut tombs and agricultural towers
- The village of Battir and its eco-museum


## 5. Important facts
- UNESCO inscribed the landscape in 2014 as “Palestine: Land of Olives and Vines – Cultural Landscape of Southern Jerusalem, Battir” and placed it on the List of World Heritage in Danger the same year. The terraces are fed by a network of channels from underground sources, and the water is shared between the village families in a time-managed rotation. UNESCO’s statement warns that building a separation barrier through the valley would severely damage the terraces and the landscape.

## 9. Visitor walkthrough
The record describes a series of farmed valleys called widian. Some terraces are irrigated for market gardens; others are dry and planted with grapevines and olive trees. Water from underground sources gathers in a collection pool and runs through channels, and the families of Battir take turns to receive it.
